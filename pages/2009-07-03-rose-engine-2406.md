<h2>The traditional view</h2>
A lathe used in plain wood turning has a rotating spindle whose position is fixed. Attached to this spindle is a standard chuck (one concentric with the spindle) used to hold the work being machined. Therefore as the spindle rotates a fixed cutting tool position will cut a circle in the workpiece concentric with the rotational center of the workpiece which is the point on the workpiece at the same position as the spindle center.

A Rose Engine is a lathe whose headstock, and therefore spindle center, is allowed to move as the spindle rotates. Therefore, unlike a lathe used for plain wood turning  a Rose Engine with a fixed cutting tool will cut a path that is not concentric with the rotational center of the workpiece. But will cut a path whose distance from the rotational center  of the workpiece  is not fixed but is dependent upon the amount and direction of the spindles deviation from its nominal central position. Typically this deviation is quite small, of the order of around 50mm maximum either side of the headstock's nominal center transversally across the bed of the lathe. This movement may be achieved either by pushing the headstock from side to side or, alternatively and more popularly, by rocking the headstock about some pivot point. The difference between the two methods is that rocking also causes a small vertical change in the spindles position whereas pushing does not.

This headstock movement is synchronized with, and mechanically connected to the rotation of the spindle using pattern templates called <a href="{{ '/software/ornamental-turning-workbench/rosette/' | relative_url }}" title="Rosette">a Rosette</a>. One or more of these Rosettes are rigidly connected to the lathe spindle so they rotate along with it.

A piece of some material, called a rubber for obvious reasons, is rigidly connected to a fixed position on the Rose Engine bed and held at spindle center height against the edge of one or more rosettes often by physically connecting the Rose Engine headstock to the stanchion holding this rubber with some form of strong tension spring.  Now as the spindle rotates so do the rosettes and as the rubber is rigidly fixed and held in contact with the edge of  one or more rosettes.  Then for any given rotational movement , as the largest radius of the group of rosettes held against the rubber decreases the headstock and thus the spindle is pulled towards the rubber by the spring.  Alternatively as the largest radius of this group of rosettes increases the spindle is pushed away from the rubber causing the spring to lengthen. Both cases result in varying the position of the center of the lathe spindle as a function of its angle of rotation.  It is this spindle movement that causes a cutter held at a fixed position on the lathe bed to cut a varying path on a workpiece held in a standard chuck connected to the spindle.

Obviously if there was only one rosette and this rosette was a circular disc concentric with the center of the spindle then no variation in radius would occur as the rosette rotated. Therefore there would be no movement of the spindle center and the cutter path would be, as in plain turning, a circle.

Any path produced on a Rose Engine is periodic because every revolution of the spindle will result in the same movement of the spindle center. However, if the position of a rosette were itself to be loosened, rotated slightly on the spindle then rigidly fixed in its new position. The resulting path after this change in phase would be the same as before except for starting and ending at a different position on the workpiece. Thus changing the angular position of  rosettes on a spindle allows one to cut different paths on the workpiece all with the same overall shape but placed at different positions. One use of the Rose Engine is to cut periodic spirograph type patterns on wood and metal.
<h2>A cnc based view</h2>
Keep the spindle center fixed and as it rotates varying the tool position we can simulate the effect of a Rose Engine.  A rosette is just a profile of radial values as a function of angle. So for any given rotational angle we can look up the corresponding rosette radius and move the table by that amount.   Of course the direction to move the table in depends upon which side of the spindle axis the rubber is situated, but that can be calculated from the rubber position. This is the principle I have used in my software.

I use three stepper motors. The first stepper is connected to the lathe spindle allowing the spindle to be rotated to any angular position.   The headstock is allowed to move along the X and Z axes controlled by the other two stepper motors. Thus all three variables, Angle of spindle, horizontal  position of cutting tool and depth of cut are under computer control.
