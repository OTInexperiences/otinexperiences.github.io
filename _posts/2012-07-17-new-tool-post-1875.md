I have become convinced that the original aluminium tool post I made for my rose engine was a little too flimsy and not rigid enough.  So I have spent the last week making a more substantial one.

It is not quite finished, the stepper needs connecting up and wiring but the main physical construction is complete. Recently I am beginning to appreciate the qualities of cast iron when it comes to damping vibrations, more mass is a good thing. Not having a ready supply of this material I began to consider making a tool post out of epoxy concrete, but then I realized that I already possessed a base I could use with a bit of a re-arrangement and re-machining.

When I originally tried to convert my wood lathe, I bought a cheap cast iron x-y table which has been lying around the workshop for the last couple of years so I decided to cannibalize this to make a base for then tool-post.

In my new design the machined bed of the x-y table would not be used so I decided to replace the aluminium cross table on my rose engine with the now redundant bed which I also hope will help to dampen vibrations as it is much heavier than the original.

So I pulled the table apart, cut off the top dovetails and milled the table level. I then used these dovetails to make two clamps to hold the table top in place on my rose engine. I attached the linear bearings together with the clamp to a piece of aluminium 10mm thick and then cut and machined down the dovetails, cut them in two   and attached each of the parts to either end of the supporting aluminium plate.  As you can see from the image below the cap head screws pressing on an iron strip which were originally used to remove any play on the slide now act as clamps.

I am very happy with the result as I now have a substantial table that I can slide and lock into different positions across the rose engine as well as towards and away from it.

I now had a Y table that was going to become the base for my tool post. The acme lead-screw used for the Y axis was held in a cast iron boss attached to one end of the table and left to float free at the other end. I have temporarily decided to re-use the lead-screw even though it is an acme thread which has a little bit of backlash, but I am prepared to swap it for a ball-screw is things do not work out.

Originally a motor was directly connected to the screw using a flexible coupling. This time I decided to mount the motor next to the slide and connect it to the screw using a timing belt and two pulleys. The ratio between the pulleys is 2 : 1 , so two complete revolutions of the motor will result in one revolution of the pulley.

An aluminium plate was fixed to the casting using the two threaded holes I previously used to mount the motor. But this time the motor would be mounted at the back of the plate alongside the slide. To facilitate this further machining of the casting was necessary to make enough room for the motor to fit.

I removed the boss holding the lead-screw in place (which was bolted on)  in order to add the aluminium plate used to support the motor,  so I had to make a new arrangement to hold the screw. I decided to sandwich the screw between two pairs of thrust bearings to hold it in place at the end connected to the motor and if necessary, hold the other end in a roller bearing instead of letting it float as it was originally.

The first thing to do was machine flat and true the inside of the end of the base so that it was perpendicular to the direction of the lead-screw. After this I bored a hole in the outside of the aluminium plate just large enough to hold the outer thrust bearing. I had to let the inner thrust bearing be exposed because I could not get a boring head into a suitable position in the casting to allow me to bore out a location for it. the plate was only thick enough to allow me to inset one thrust bearing and the actual casting wall was only a few mm thick at this point.  Fortunately the spindle was 10mm diameter and as I already had two 10mm bore thrust bearings so all I needed to do was re-machine the lead-screw to lengthen the spindle on one end to accommodate this new arrangement.

The image above shows a section through the driving end, the blue is the spindle, the yellow is the casting wall, the light gray is the aluminium plate, The green items are the thrust bearings and the dark gray is the timing pulley.  The inner orange item is a brass spacer and the outer orange item is a brass threaded nut containing a grub screw. The spindle now sticks out of the casting and so the last thing to do is cut it down to size.

Then, I needed something to surface the top of the casting which is only a few mm thick. My friend Peter gave me a substantial piece of steel plate which is 1 inch thick and this does the job nicely as well as adding much needed mass. I had to do a bit of machining to get the plate to size, and I had to machine a corner off to clear the stepper motor but that is all.

Finally at last weeks SOT quarterly meeting I picked up a Myford lathe slide which you see bolted to the top of the plate. This makes an ideal vertical adjustment to get my cutting frame on centre.  I can position this slide at either side of the table and is just the thing to complete the tool post. If I need to position it elsewhere in the future I just need to drill and tap two holes.

Now I just have to make a couple of bits to allow me to bolt my cutting frame to the slide. I think that I now have a solid tool table which should work a treat.

So to the future and dealing with backlash. If it proves to be too much my first approach will be to connect a small frame to the nut and pack this with heat softened poly-morph plastic and let it harden to exactly match the thread . If this does not work, I will try to reuse the acme thread I have on the original tool post I made which has a very close fitting nut. However that screw is a much smaller diameter so will require me to re-machine some of the parts. Finally if all else fails I will buy a ball-screw and nut.
